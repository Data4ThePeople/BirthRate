The rural fertility reversal

In 1991 American women in metropolitan counties had children at a higher rate than women in rural ones. Thirty-three years later the ranking has flipped and the gap is wide. This is a map of where the birth rate actually fell — and it fell hardest in the biggest cities.

Two clocks

The national rate and the metro–rural gap move on completely different schedules. Between 1991 and 2007 the national rate dipped, recovered, and arrived back where it started — sixteen years of nothing. Almost the entire fall in the level has come since. The gap underneath it did no such thing: it widened at a steady pace right through the flat years, and was already half open before the level moved at all.

A gradient, not a divide

The decline is not simply "cities versus countryside". Ranked along the USDA's rural–urban continuum, the size of the fall tracks settlement density almost monotonically: the denser the county, the further fertility fell.

It is not people moving

Women of childbearing age did move to cities: non-metro counties held — of American women aged 15–44 in 1991 and — by 2024. But that redistribution explains almost none of the national decline. Splitting the change in the national rate into the part from rates falling within groups and the part from women moving between them settles it.

Migration between rural and urban America is close to irrelevant to the national total, because in 1991 the places women moved to had the higher birth rates. The decline happened inside places — and most of all inside the largest ones.

How this was built

Public US birth data has a hard geographic floor: NCHS removes all geography from natality microdata after 2004, and CDC WONDER identifies only counties of 100,000+ people, pooling everything smaller into an unnamed remainder. A map built that way leaves most of rural America blank — fatal for this question.

The way around it is the Census Bureau's Population Estimates Program, which publishes an annual birth count for every county as an input to its population estimates. Those counts come from the same vital records but are not suppressed. Paired with SEER's county population by age and sex, they give a complete county fertility panel from 1991 to 2024.

Estimate years, not calendar years

PEP birth years run July to June. Aligning our series to a July–June blend of published NCHS calendar-year totals cuts the median discrepancy from 0.63% to 0.28%, confirming the offset is the window and not an error.

Two interpolated years

Every PEP vintage reports a partial quarter in its launch year, so no published county file covers estimate years 2000 and 2010. Both are linearly interpolated and flagged; 2020 is recovered from the vintage-2020 release.

Merged geographies

Alaska, Connecticut and Hawaii are held as single statewide units, and Broomfield County is merged with the four Colorado counties it was carved from in 2001, because the sources cannot be reconciled below that level. Alaska is excluded from the metro/non-metro series, where a single code would be misleading.

What standardisation does and does not remove

The age-standardised measure removes the effect of the local female age structure. It does not remove differences in who lives where — education, income, nativity, marital status. Those remain open questions, not answered here.
