County fertility · United States · 1982–2024

The rural fertility gap

In 1982 rural American women had children at a far higher rate than women in big cities — a gap of eight births per thousand. By 1994 it had not just closed but inverted. Then it opened again, the other way round, and is now nearly as wide as it was at the start. Over forty-three years the rural–urban fertility gradient has run in both directions.

Two clocks

The national rate and the metro–rural gap move on completely different schedules. For twenty-five years the national rate simply oscillated: 67 in 1982, a peak of 71 in 1990, a trough of 64 in 1998, back to 69 by 2007. No trend at all, and then a cliff. The gap underneath it was never still — it fell through the whole of the 1980s, bottomed in the mid-1990s, and has climbed ever since. Neither turning point lines up with the other.

The gradient ran both ways

Ranked along the USDA's rural–urban continuum, each era produces a clean monotonic gradient — pointing in opposite directions, at nearly mirrored magnitudes. Through 1994 the fall got steeper the more rural the county, and the largest metros actually gained. Since 1994 the ordering is exactly inverted: the largest metros have fallen furthest, and the most remote rural counties have gained. Whatever reorganised American fertility did not simply run from city to country. It changed sign.

What broke in the 1980s

The first half of the story — rural fertility falling away while metro held flat — has a shape to it. Grouping rural counties by what their economies ran on in the late 1970s, the collapse tracks commodity exposure. Mining counties fell furthest, farming counties next, and rural counties built on manufacturing, government or retirement fell about as much as the country as a whole. Those are the two sectors that a soaring dollar, twenty percent interest rates and a global price crash hit hardest, and they are the two that show up here.

Counties are classified on their 1975–79 income shares, before either bust. Later editions of this classification move counties out of the category once the industry leaves, which would quietly erase the effect being looked for.

It is not people moving

Women of childbearing age did move to cities: non-metro counties held — of American women aged 15–44 in 1982 and — by 2024. But that redistribution explains almost none of the national decline. Splitting the change in the national rate into the part from rates falling within groups and the part from women moving between them settles it.

Migration between rural and urban America accounts for about three percent of the national decline. Fertility fell inside every kind of county; where women lived barely moved the total.

How this was built

Public US birth data has a hard geographic floor: NCHS removes all geography from natality microdata after 2004, and CDC WONDER identifies only counties of 100,000+ people, pooling everything smaller into an unnamed remainder. A map built that way leaves most of rural America blank — fatal for this question.

The way around it is the Census Bureau's Population Estimates Program, which publishes an annual birth count for every county as an input to its population estimates. Those counts come from the same vital records but are not suppressed. Paired with SEER's county population by age and sex, they give a complete county fertility panel from 1991 to 2024, which natality microdata extends back to 1982.

Two sources, one series

1982–1990 comes from NCHS natality microdata, which names every county and reproduces published national totals exactly. 1991–2024 comes from Census PEP. On 1995, 1998 and 2001, where both name the same 457 large counties, the median county-level ratio is within about 1%, and its sign tracks the direction of the national trend each year — the signature of PEP's July–June year rather than a difference in what is counted.

Two interpolated years

Every PEP vintage reports a partial quarter in its launch year, so no published county file covers estimate years 2000 and 2010. Both are linearly interpolated and flagged; 2020 is recovered from the vintage-2020 release.

Merged geographies

Alaska, Connecticut and Hawaii are held as single statewide units, and Broomfield County is merged with the four Colorado counties it was carved from in 2001, because the sources cannot be reconciled below that level. Alaska is excluded from the metro/non-metro series, where a single code would be misleading.

What standardisation does and does not remove

The age-standardised measure removes the effect of the local female age structure. It does not remove differences in who lives where — education, income, nativity, marital status. Those remain open questions, not answered here.
